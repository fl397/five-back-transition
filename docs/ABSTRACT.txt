Five Back: When Does Transition End?

Introduction
Early offense is typically defined by elapsed time, but elapsed time alone does not tell us whether the defense has numerically recovered. Prior tracking work quantified the trade-off between offensive rebounding and transition defense (Wiens, Balakrishnan, Brooks and Guttag, MIT Sloan 2013); the defensive state at the moment of a transition shot has not been measured directly. We measure that state, ask how long five-player recovery takes, and how much early rim access is associated with recovery state rather than the clock.

Methods
We use 631 NBA games from 2015-16 with player-tracking and play-by-play data joined under a validated timing correction. Possessions begin with a defensive rebound or a live-ball turnover (48,083 starts). At every frame we count defenders inside their own half, N_BACK, and define FIVE_BACK when all five are back. The unit is the first field-goal attempt of each possession (32,835 possessions), with FIVE_BACK read one second before the shot. The primary outcome is a rim attempt (within 4 ft). Linear probability models adjust for elapsed possession time (restricted cubic spline), start type, period and score margin, with game-clustered uncertainty. All rules were committed before outcomes were opened.

Results
The recovery clock is slow and dispersed: it takes a median 5.63 s for all five defenders to get back (interquartile range 4.82-6.39 s); only 56.6% of possessions have five back by 6 s, and 10.4% still lack a fifth defender at 8 s. With all five defenders back, the first shot is 17.3 percentage points less likely to be a rim attempt (95% CI -19.3 to -15.3), similarly after rebounds (-16.4) and turnovers (-19.8). Same clock, different state: among shots taken 6-7 s into the possession, rim-attempt rates were 59.5% with fewer than five defenders back and 38.4% with five. Elapsed time alone implies a 42.0-point difference in rim-attempt probability between shots taken 2 s and 10 s into the possession; adding FIVE_BACK attenuates that model-estimated gradient to 23.5 points, by 44%, while 56% remains after accounting for five-back status. The association is monotone: relative to five back, rim-attempt probability is 8.4 points higher with four defenders back, 14.5 with three, and 29.3 with two or fewer.

Conclusion
Transition is better characterized by defensive recovery state than by the clock alone: N_BACK and T5, the time to five-player recovery, make that state measurable from tracking data. Five back is not the same as a set defense: numerical recovery accounts for a substantial part of the early-possession rim gradient, but the remaining 56% is not explained here. These are associations in one historical public season; they do not identify why any shot is taken. For practice, N_BACK can label the defensive state of each transition shot and T5 provides a possession-level recovery clock, letting analysts compare transition opportunities at the same defensive state rather than by elapsed time alone.
